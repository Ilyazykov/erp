Crypto on exchanges and wallets becomes buys / sells / rewards from their transaction logs (coins bought by card through Mercuryo / Transak at what the card paid, on the day they arrived), plus one reconciling row per coin dated today so its quantity matches the current balance (transfers between own wallets and network fees aren't trades). The full export with fewer custom holdings and the money added. Invested vs earned

The investments only -- shares, bonds, funds, deposits, savings accounts, gold, ЦФА and crypto (money on cards, cash and real estate stay out). Invested: your own money put in -- buys less sells and redemptions, top-ups of deposits and savings less withdrawals, money and coins brought onto the crypto exchanges / wallets less taken out -- each at that day's rate; dividends, coupons, interest and a bank's payouts for investing (Alfa's promotions) that were reinvested are not your money and stay out. Value: what it was worth that day. Both less what's owed on the credit cards that day (money borrowed, much of it moved onto savings accounts), so they're own money; the gap is untouched. The gap is what was earned: price growth, exchange rates, dividends, coupons, interest. With a period chosen -- or a day set in "count from" (or clicked on the chart) -- invested starts from the value on that first day and adds only what was put in after it, so the gap is what that period earned. Worked out in the database with every capital rebuild.

Earned over time

The gap between the two lines above: what the investments had earned by each day, from the same day counted from to the zoom's end. % is that over the capital that worked on average (Modified Dietz: the value at the start plus each put-in / taken-out weighted by how much of the period was left after it).

Earned by asset

Over the same period as the chart above (from the day counted from, or the zoom's start, to its end): each asset's value at the end less its value at the start and the money put in between -- dividends, coupons and interest count as earned. % is that over the capital that worked on average (Modified Dietz): the value at the start plus each put-in and taken-out weighted by how much of the asset's own time was left after it (from the period start, or its first buy, to the period end, or its last sale) -- money back early (amortisation, a sale) counts for less. An asset is its ticker across accounts; crypto goes coin by coin (a trade on an exchange moves money from one coin to another), stablecoins (USDT, USDC, DAI...) are dollars: one row, USD, with an exchange's USD Earn.

What the funds hold

Shares held directly plus each fund's share of its own holdings (fund value × weight), matched by ISIN across funds — the real exposure to each company. Fund lists come from the issuers (monthly refresh); TRND's is from its February 2026 report.

EXPERIMENTAL — US market-wide Equity Risk Premium, using Robert Shiller's own published Excess CAPE Yield (solid, monthly, 1871–present, updated directly from his maintained spreadsheet — not recomputed from this site's 37-ticker basket). Excess CAPE Yield = (1 / CAPE) − real long-term interest rate, i.e. the S&P 500's cyclically-adjusted earnings yield minus the real 10-year rate — conceptually the same idea as the Russian portfolio's Layer 1 (Portfolio EY − OFZ 10Y), just for the whole US market. This is NOT the same figure as Damodaran's Implied ERP (T12m) (dotted line — a different, DCF-based methodology): a genuine monthly series Damodaran has recomputed and published every month since September 2008, using trailing-12-month dividends + buybacks against the current S&P 500 level and Treasury rate (i.e. NOT his once-a-year headline figure held flat — it moves every month on its own data). Full Shiller history (1871–present) is loaded; drag the range slider below the chart, or the box below it, to zoom into any period — it opens on the last 10 years (Damodaran's series only goes back to 2008-09).

Z-score of Shiller's Excess CAPE Yield vs its own 36-month rolling history (computed on the full 1871–present series). Above +1.5 → stocks unusually cheap vs (real) rates → historically a buy signal. Below −1.5 → stocks unusually expensive vs rates → historically a trim signal. This is the Z-score that drives the allocation donut below.

INFORMATIONAL ONLY — the same 36-month rolling Z-score, computed instead on Damodaran's Implied ERP (T12m) and on its own (much shorter, 2008-09–present) history. A second, independent read of the same "cheap vs expensive" question, using forward-looking DCF assumptions rather than Shiller's backward-looking earnings yield — shown for comparison only. It does not feed into the allocation donut below, which uses Shiller's Z-score only.
